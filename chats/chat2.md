I read through your business model slides, the AI advantage slide, and pulled some numbers on Too Good To Go. Here's my honest take on each of his points — I think he's right on two of three, and the third is more nuanced than he's framing it.

---

## Point 1: The missing 10% commission — half right

**What I removed and why:** Earlier drafts had a 10% commission on every sale. I replaced it with the invisible consumer-side convenience fee so we could lead with **"Free for retailers"** — which is genuinely a strategic moat. Groupon's reputation among independents is poisonous precisely because they took 30–50%, and we want to be the un-Groupon.

**But — he's right that we left money on the table.** Look at how Too Good To Go actually does it: they charge a $1.79 fixed fee per order plus a $89 annual fee deducted from the partner's profits. Bags retail at $3.99/$4.99/$5.99 and TGTG takes $1.79 off each — that's effectively **30–45% of the bag price**, not 10%. And retailers still queue up to join.

**My recommendation:** Don't bring back a 10% commission (it kills the headline). Instead, replace it with a **small fixed success fee per redeemed claim** — say £0.99–£1.49 — payable by the retailer on actual completed transactions only. That gives us:

| Model | £40 haircut yields | Retailer feels it as | Headline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old: 10% commission | £4.00 | "I'm being taxed" | Lost |
| Current: consumer fee only | ~£1.20 | Nothing | "Free for retailers" |
| **Proposed: £1.29 success fee** | **£1.29** | "Pay-per-result advertising" | **"Free to join. Pay only when it works."** |

That's roughly the same per-transaction economics as the original 10% on small tickets, scales sensibly, and is **easier to defend** than a percentage. Investors will recognise the TGTG playbook immediately.

---

## Point 2: The pricing direction — he's 100% right, and it's a real comms problem

The deck currently says "Dynamic pricing has worked for airlines and hotels for thirty years" and shows AI that "ramps the discount as expiry closes." Those two sentences contradict each other. Airlines mostly **raise** prices last-minute; we're doing the opposite.

The correct mental model is **yield management for perishable inventory** — the same logic HotelTonight, Last Minute.com and standby airline fares use: an empty 3pm chair, a 6pm yoga slot, a Tuesday dinner table all expire worthless at the end of the day, so the price *drops* as the window closes to capture any willing buyer.

**Recommended fixes:**
- Rewrite slide 8 headline to something like "Reverse-surge pricing. Discounts deepen as the clock ticks."
- Swap the airlines/hotels line for: "The same yield management hotels and standby airline tickets use — applied to the high street for the first time."
- Add a small chart on that slide: a downward-stepped price line over a 4-hour window (10% → 25% → 40% → 60%) to make the direction unambiguous.

---

## Point 3: Delivery — I'd push back, but gently

His instinct is commercial — delivery is huge. But every category we serve has a reason it *can't* be delivered:
- **Services** (60%+ of categories — hair, beauty, fitness, treatments): physically impossible
- **Restaurants**: covered to death by Deliveroo/UberEats already; we'd be a worse version of them
- **Retail products**: real opportunity, but only ~30% of our supply

Adding delivery as a core pillar would muddy our story (we're the **footfall** app, not another delivery app). My suggestion: add a single line on the product slide reading **"Delivery optional, via Stuart / Uber Direct partner integration — Phase 2."** That answers the question without diluting the pitch.
